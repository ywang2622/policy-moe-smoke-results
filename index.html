Can a learned router choose the right policy expert?

We ran a small, source-grounded PolicyMoE pipeline on five U.S. Department of Energy documents using Qwen2.5-7B-Instruct. It worked end to end. The dataset is far too small to claim a winner.

What happened?

The shared LoRA answered one of two Level 1 questions correctly; the other systems missed both. Every system answered the two Level 2 items and the objective Level 3 item correctly. With counts this small, the responsible conclusion is simply that training and inference completed—not that one architecture is better.

Learned routing

The router chose an expert from the question alone.

No known level label was used at inference. A trained linear layer scored the frozen base-model representation, applied softmax, and loaded only the highest-scoring adapter.

From documents to answers

What we actually built

The raw source format and core three-expert idea follow the paper’s official release. The questions and implementation here are our own small execution fixture.

  1. 01

    Analyze policy documents

    Five DOE documents from the paper repository’s released policy archive.

  2. 02

    Author three-level questions

    Facts, understanding, and applied scenarios; 30 total, grouped by source.

  3. 03

    Split without document leakage

    Four documents for training and one entire document held out for testing.

  4. 04

    Train adapters and router

    One shared LoRA, three specialist LoRAs, and a learned linear top-1 router.

  5. 05

    Compare consistently

    Frozen Qwen, shared LoRA, and PolicyMoE used the same six test questions.

Known limitations

What this does not prove

  • No statistical significance or architecture superiority.
  • No replication of the paper’s full 21K-question benchmark.
  • No two-judge score for the open Level 3 response yet.
  • Questions were authored and editorially reviewed, not generated in a logged live model run.
  • Distractor traces are reconstructed fixtures, not original generation logs.
